## 8.9.1 Energy-conversion architecture

Aegis-40 converts **125 MWth** of core thermal power into **40 MWe (net)** through a single-reheat-free
**regenerative superheated Rankine cycle** on the secondary side of the integral once-through steam
generator (OTSG). The primary loop is **natural-circulation** (no reactor coolant pumps), so the only
rotating primary machinery is eliminated and the entire nuclear steam supply system is contained inside
the reactor pressure vessel (see the integral-RPV model, §8.1 / `cad/aegis_rpv.step`). Heat crosses the
pressure boundary once, in the helical-coil OTSG, and never returns — there is no intermediate loop on
the power-conversion path.

The secondary flow path (Figure 8.9-1) is:

> OTSG (superheated steam) → **high-pressure (HP) turbine** → **moisture separator (MS)** →
> **low-pressure (LP) turbine** → **condenser** → condensate pump → **deaerator (open FWH-2)** →
> booster pump → **HP feedwater heater (open FWH-1)** → main feed pump → back to the OTSG.

Two open feedwater heaters and a crossover moisture separator are the only secondary heat-management
components beyond the turbine, condenser and pumps — a deliberately simple balance of plant appropriate
to a 40 MWe SMR.

**A single turbine-generator.** The HP and LP stages and the crossover moisture separator are mounted on
**one shaft driving one generator** — a *single tandem-compound turbine-generator* (T-1), which is the
conventional arrangement for an LWR SMR of this rating. This is a deliberate simplification over a
two-turbine (separate HP/LP machine) layout: one casing line, one shaft train, one generator, one
lubrication/control system and one foundation reduce capital cost, building volume and commissioning
complexity, and remove a cross-coupling between machines — material gains in construction ease and
economics at 40 MWe. The district-heat and hydrogen off-takes below add **no** further turbine: the
thermochemical store is charged from a steam *extraction* (not a back-pressure pass-out machine), so the
single-turbine architecture is preserved end-to-end.

**Figure 8.9-1** (`cycle/plant_pfd_seawater_h2.png`, generated by `scripts/plant_pfd_seawater_h2.py`) is

## 8.9.2 Cycle configuration and design choices

| Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Net electric output | **40.0 MWe** |
| Core thermal power (= secondary heat in) | 125 MWth |
| Turbine-inlet (OTSG) steam pressure | **4.5 MPa** |
| Turbine-inlet steam temperature | **296 °C** |
| Steam mass flow | 57.8 kg/s |
| Condenser pressure | 7 kPa (Tsat 39 °C) |
| HP extraction → FWH-1 | 1.0 MPa |
| Crossover / MS + deaerator | 0.15 MPa |
| Turbine isentropic efficiency | 0.85 |
| Pump isentropic efficiency | 0.82 |
| Generator × mechanical | 0.985 |
| Balance-of-plant house load | 5.0 % of gross |

**Why a moisture separator.** Expanding near-saturated steam from 4.5 MPa straight to the 7 kPa condenser
would leave the last turbine stage at ~22 % moisture — far past the ~12 % blade-erosion limit. A
moisture separator at the 0.15 MPa crossover dries the steam to saturated vapour before the final LP
stage, holding **last-stage moisture to 10.8 % (exhaust quality x = 0.892)**, within accepted practice.
The separated liquid drains to the deaerator at the same pressure.

**Why regeneration.** The two open feedwater heaters raise the feedwater temperature from 39 °C
(condenser) to 180 °C before the OTSG, lifting the mean temperature of heat addition and the cycle
efficiency from ~29 % (simple Rankine) to **33.7 % (gross)** — the difference between missing and meeting
the 40 MWe target. They also deaerate the feedwater (corrosion control) and reduce OTSG economizer duty,
which improves the OTSG pinch (§8.9.4).

**Second-law (exergy) analysis.** A first-law efficiency alone does not show *where* the
conversion potential is lost. An exergy balance referenced to a dead state of T₀ = 25 °C,
P₀ = 0.101 MPa — with the heat source valued at the primary-coolant log-mean temperature
(282.6 °C, i.e. the exergy of the 308 → 258 °C primary legs) — locates each irreversibility
(Table 8.9-3b; `scripts/thermo_exergy.py` → `cycle/cycle_exergy.csv`). Of the **57.9 MW** of
exergy delivered by the primary coolant, **39.7 MW** leaves as net electricity, giving an
**exergetic (second-law) efficiency of 68.5 %** — the figure of merit that the 31.8 % first-law
number cannot convey, since the remaining ~31 % is split between identifiable,
partially-recoverable losses.

**Table 8.9-3b — Exergy (second-law) balance, referenced to the primary-coolant heat-exergy:**

| Component | Exergy destruction / loss | Share of supplied exergy |
|---|---:|---:|
| Turbine + generator stages | 6.02 MW | 10.4 % |
| OTSG / steam generator (finite-ΔT) | 3.95 MW | 6.8 % |
| Condenser heat rejected to sea (external loss) | 3.70 MW | 6.4 % |
| Generator + BOP house load (external loss) | 2.77 MW | 4.8 % |
| Deaerator (FWH-2) | 0.97 MW | 1.7 % |
| FWH-1 | 0.81 MW | 1.4 % |
| Condenser internal ΔT + pumps + moisture separator | ~0.04 MW | ~0.1 % |
| **Total destroyed + lost** | **18.3 MW** | **31.5 %** |
| **Useful electric output** | **39.7 MW** | **68.5 %** |

The exergy map is diagnostically useful: the largest *internal* irreversibility is the turbine
expansion (10.4 %, set by the 0.85 isentropic efficiency), followed by the OTSG finite-ΔT heat
transfer (6.8 %, the price of the once-through counterflow approach in §8.9.4); the condenser
and generator/BOP terms (6.4 % + 4.8 %) are *external* losses to the sea sink and house load
rather than thermodynamic destruction. The feedwater heaters together destroy only ~3 % — the
regenerative train is well-matched. This identifies the turbine and OTSG approach temperatures
as the only levers worth pursuing for further efficiency gain.

**Ultimate heat sink — seawater once-through, and its sea-temperature impact.** The 82.6 MWth
condenser duty is rejected to the **Black Sea (Sinop coastal site)** by a **once-through seawater**
circuit — *not* a wet cooling tower — so there is **no consumptive (evaporative) freshwater use**.
For a design condenser rise ΔT_cond ≤ 10 K the intake is ṁ = Q/(c_p·ΔT) = 82.6 MW/(4.00 kJ/kg·K · 10 K)
≈ **2,065 kg/s (≈ 2.0 m³/s)** of brackish seawater (≈18 psu; c_p ≈ 4.00 kJ/kg·K). Summer is the
absolute-temperature-limiting case: at a 25 °C intake the outfall is 35 °C; throttling to ΔT=7 K
(ṁ ≈ 2,950 kg/s) caps it at 32 °C. The **environmental** metric is the *far-field* excess after
near-field mixing: a submerged **multiport diffuser** achieves 10–50× dilution within tens of metres,
reducing the +10 K outfall to **0.9 K (10×) → 0.2 K (50×)**, well within the typical **≤ 3 K**
edge-of-mixing-zone limit (met at only ~3× dilution). With a modest Black Sea longshore current
(0.05–0.20 m/s) the far-field rise is **0.05–0.20 K**, negligible against the natural seasonal swing
(8 → 25 °C). Sizing and the dilution sensitivity are computed in `scripts/seawater_heat_sink.py`
(→ `cycle/seawater_heat_sink.csv`, `cycle/seawater_plume_dilution.png`).

## 8.9.4 Primary–secondary coupling: OTSG feasibility

Because the OTSG transfers the full 125 MWth across a counterflow once-through surface, the secondary
steam conditions are **not free** — they are bounded by the §8.4 primary leg temperatures (308 °C hot,
258 °C cold). Two checks were applied (Table 8.9-4):

- **Superheater approach** = (hot leg − steam) = 308 − 296 = **12 °C** (> 0, feasible). The OTSG
  superheats steam to 296 °C against the 308 °C hot leg.
- **Evaporator pinch** = (primary temperature where the secondary starts to boil − secondary saturation
  temperature). Because the economizer pre-heats the feedwater, the secondary saturation temperature
  (**257 °C**) sits just above the 258 °C cold leg; the primary at the start-of-boiling point is ~266 °C,
  giving a pinch of **8.8 °C** — a practical value for a helical-coil OTSG.

This is the binding constraint on steam pressure: pushing steam much above 4.5 MPa drops the pinch below
the ~8 °C practical floor (e.g. 4.8 MPa → 5.3 °C). The selected **4.5 MPa** keeps a healthy pinch while
holding the 40 MWe-class output. The re-coupling to the §8.4 legs is computed in
`scripts/thermo_cycle_recouple.py`; the design model (`scripts/thermo_cycle.py`) recomputes the pinch
automatically.

**Table 8.9-4 — OTSG feasibility (primary 308 → 258 °C):**

| Check | Value | Criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Secondary saturation temperature | 257.4 °C | economizer-limited (cold leg 258 °C) |
| Superheater hot-end approach | 12.0 °C | > 0 |
| Evaporator pinch ΔT | 8.8 °C | ≳ 8 °C (practical OTSG) |

## 8.9.5 Thermochemical energy storage (TCES) for district heating

Aegis-40 is a **polygeneration** plant: alongside the 40 MWe of electricity it supplies **district
heating (DH)** through a **thermochemical energy store (TCES)** and **hydrogen** through off-peak
electrolysis (§8.9.6). The store decouples DH *delivery* from the near-constant ~100 % reactor power
and — unlike a sensible-heat (hot-fluid) store — holds its charge **loss-free** because the energy resides
in reversible chemical bonds, not in a hot mass. This makes multi-day and even **seasonal** heat storage
practical (summer charge → winter heat) with negligible self-discharge.

**Architecture — TCES as an IHX-isolated, non-safety auxiliary (Figure 8.9-3).** The store is located
**outside the nuclear island** and is coupled to the steam cycle only through an **intermediate heat
exchanger (IHX)**. On **charge**, HP-extraction steam (1.0 MPa, 180 °C) gives up heat across the IHX to a
**closed charge loop** (~168 °C) that regenerates the store; on **discharge**, the store's reaction heat
is delivered to a **separate district-heating water loop (90 / 45 °C)**. The IHX is a deliberate
safety-classification and chemistry boundary: the storage medium (ammonia or adsorbed water) never shares
a fluid path with the reactor steam, so the TCES carries **no chemical- or water-quality risk into the
power-conversion cycle** and is classified as a **non-safety auxiliary**. Figure 8.9-3
(`cycle/pfd_cogeneration_tces.png`, `scripts/pfd_cogeneration_tces.py`) shows the single-turbine cycle,
the IHX isolation, the charge/discharge loops, the store and the H₂ electrolyser.

**Storage mission, not electricity time-shift.** The store's purpose is **low-grade heat for district
heating**, so it is sized and graded for a ~90 °C network, *not* for regenerating power-grade steam.
This is why a thermochemical store (high density, near-zero standby loss) is the correct choice over a
sensible two-tank store, and why high-temperature (~300–350 °C) metal-hydride / metal-oxide stores were
*rejected*: delivering 300 °C heat to a 90 °C network destroys exergy with no benefit.

**Two candidate media (Table 8.9-5).** Two reversible solid–gas systems match the DH grade and are
carried side-by-side:

- **Ammine resorption — NiCl₂–SrCl₂ / NH₃** (primary). A two-salt resorption pair cycling ammonia
  between a high-temperature reactor (HTR, primary salt) and a low-temperature reactor (LTR, secondary
  salt):
  > charge (endothermic): NiCl₂·6NH₃ + heat → NiCl₂·2NH₃ + 4 NH₃↑   (HTR desorption)
  > discharge (exothermic): NiCl₂·2NH₃ + 4 NH₃↑ → NiCl₂·6NH₃ + heat  (HTR adsorption, ~150 °C)
  >
  > with SrCl₂·8NH₃ ⇌ SrCl₂·NH₃ + 7 NH₃ regenerating the ammonia in the LTR.
  Energy density **1.3–2.6 MJ/kg** of salt and heat-storage efficiency up to **0.98** (Yan et al. 2020).
  Highest density → most compact store; the natural choice where volume dominates (seasonal).

- **Zeolite-13X / water adsorption** (ammonia-free alternative):
  > charge (endothermic): zeolite·(H₂O) + heat → dry zeolite + H₂O↑
  > discharge (exothermic): dry zeolite + H₂O↑ → zeolite·(H₂O) + heat  (~130 °C)
  Lower density (~0.20 kWh/kg) but uses only water — a simpler safety and public-acceptance story on a
  nuclear site; demonstrated for district heating (Dahlberg et al.).

**Sizing and the charge penalty (Table 8.9-5; `scripts/tces_dh_balance.py`).** For a reference duty of a
**25 MWth peak / 200 MWh_th daily DH block** (90 / 45 °C network), the ammine store is **735 t / 735 m³**
versus **1000 t / 1538 m³** for zeolite — the ammine is **≈2.1× more compact**. Charging draws
regeneration-grade (168 °C) heat from HP extraction, which costs **0.31 MWe per MWth charged**: during the
~16 h off-peak charge window the net output dips ~4.4 MWe (40 → ~35.6 MWe), while during the peak
discharge the full 40 MWe is sent to grid **and** 25 MWth flows from the store to the DH network with no
live-steam penalty. This electricity-for-time-shift trade is the honest price of holding the high-grade
heat needed to regenerate a chemical store, and is the dominant figure of merit for the dispatch schedule.

## 8.9.6 Hydrogen co-generation — off-peak (night-shift)

As the second integrated off-take, **off-peak (night-shift) electricity** feeds a water-electrolysis
unit, converting otherwise low-value night-time output into storable hydrogen and raising effective
plant utilisation. The reactor stays at constant power; during low grid demand the electricity is
routed to the electrolyser instead of the grid. A high-temperature **solid-oxide electrolysis (SOE)**
stack can additionally use secondary-side steam to **pre-heat** the feed, lowering the electrical demand
per kg H₂ — but the bulk of the splitting energy is electrical, so a lower-temperature PEM/alkaline unit
is an equally valid baseline (our secondary steam is ~296 °C, well below the ~700–850 °C an SOE stack
ideally wants, so the steam contributes feed-preheat, not the full high-temperature benefit). A
reference **8 MWe PEM** block at ~50 kWh/kg produces **≈160 kg H₂/h (≈640 t/yr at 4 000 off-peak h)**;
DH-grade heat from the TCES discharge can pre-heat the electrolyser feed. The electrolyser technology and
capacity are set with [LAY]. Definition, analysis and plant connection are developed in `systems/soe/`.
